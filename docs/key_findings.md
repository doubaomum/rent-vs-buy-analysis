# Key Findings

This document summarizes the main results of the buy-versus-rent analysis for Canada. The project answers two connected questions using two kinds of data: a **macro comparison** of housing against equity markets over 1990–2025 (real, inflation-adjusted indices), and a **dollar-based rent-versus-buy simulation** over 2005–2025 (nominal CAD).

**Bottom line:** As a raw asset, Canadian housing was the weakest of the three studied. Yet under base assumptions, owning narrowly beat renting in all seven major cities over 2005–2025. The advantage was real but narrow, conditional, and reversible — driven far more by *how long the buyer held* and *what the renter invested in* than by housing appreciation or financing terms.

---

## 1. Headline Results

| Dimension | Finding |
|---|---|
| Strongest raw asset (1990–2025) | S&P 500 — 7.0% real CAGR |
| Base-case rent-vs-buy winner (2005–2025) | Owner — narrowly, in all 7 cities |
| Most owner-favourable city | Vancouver (+291 average indexed wealth gap) |
| Most renter-competitive city | Edmonton (−10 — the only negative gap) |
| Dominant risk factor | Renter portfolio choice (swing up to ~811–960 indexed points) |
| Biggest driver of the ownership advantage | Holding period |
| National CAGR (owner vs renter) | 13.5% vs 13.2% — a near-tie |

---

## 2. Macro Comparison: Housing vs. Equities (1990–2025)

On a real, inflation-adjusted basis, equities clearly outgrew housing over 35 years.

| Asset | Real CAGR (1990–2025) |
|---|---:|
| S&P 500 | 7.0% |
| TSX | 3.9% |
| Canada Housing | 2.0% |

U.S. equities delivered the strongest real growth. Canadian housing was more stable but produced the lowest price appreciation of the three — and that figure reflects price appreciation only, excluding shelter value and the leverage effects that matter to an actual owner.

**Timing mattered more than asset choice.** Across 28 entry-year / holding-period scenarios, the S&P 500 won 20, with housing and the TSX winning only 4 each. Housing won outright only for the **2000 entry cohort** — buying just before the dot-com crash beat equities for two full decades — which underscores that *when* you bought, not just *what* you bought, decided the winner.

## 3. Regional Housing (1999–2025)

Real housing growth varied meaningfully by city.

| City | Real CAGR (1999–2025) |
|---|---:|
| Vancouver | 4.1% |
| Montreal | 4.0% |
| Toronto | 3.6% |
| Canada | 3.4% |
| Ottawa | 3.3% |
| Calgary | 2.7% |
| Edmonton | 2.6% |

Vancouver and Montreal led real housing growth. Toronto showed the most visible boom-and-correction pattern, while Calgary and Edmonton had weaker long-run growth and more cyclical behaviour.

**Against equities, regional housing rarely competed.** Across 15 regional entry/holding scenarios, the S&P 500 won 8 — and every entry from 2010 onward. Regional housing won only for pre-crisis entries (2000 and 2005). Vancouver was the most durable housing winner — the only market to beat equities across 15-, 20-, and 25-year holds from a single entry year (still 4.3% real after 25 years from 2000). No city's housing beat the S&P 500 for any entry after 2005, and the TSX never won a single scenario.

## 4. Rent vs. Buy — Base Case (2005–2025)

Under base assumptions (20% down, 5-year fixed, 25-year amortization, 100% TSX renter portfolio, 100% renter discipline), owning won — but only just at the national level.

Nationally, owning and renting were a virtual tie: a final owner net-worth index of 1,264 versus 1,183 for renters, or 13.5% versus 13.2% CAGR. The story behind that near-draw is a crossover in costs over time.

**Why ownership pulled ahead: rents compounded, ownership costs didn't.** Renters started with the cheaper monthly cost (national average rent of ~1.10K versus ownership cost of ~1.43K), but rents grew **3.6% a year versus just 0.8% for ownership costs** — more than four times faster. Ownership costs stayed nearly flat until the post-2020 rate hikes, while rents climbed steadily and accelerated after 2020, eroding the renter's early advantage.

On an equal-capital basis, owning won in **all seven markets**:

| City | Winner | Owner Net-Worth Index | Renter Net-Worth Index | Owner CAGR | Renter CAGR | CAGR Gap |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Vancouver | Owner | 1,581 | 665 | 14.8% | 9.9% | 4.9 |
| Toronto | Owner | 1,389 | 1,062 | 14.1% | 12.5% | 1.5 |
| Montreal | Owner | 1,261 | 1,045 | 13.5% | 12.5% | 1.1 |
| Calgary | Owner | 1,145 | 948 | 13.0% | 11.9% | 1.1 |
| Ottawa | Owner | 1,141 | 1,060 | 12.9% | 12.5% | 0.4 |
| Edmonton | Owner | 1,025 | 925 | 12.3% | 11.8% | 0.6 |
| Canada | Owner | 1,264 | 1,183 | 13.5% | 13.2% | 0.4 |

Vancouver had by far the widest margin — a 4.9-point CAGR gap that compounds to roughly 2.4× the renter's final wealth. Ottawa and Edmonton were near-ties (0.4–0.6 points), where renting stayed competitive.

---

## 5. What Drives the Outcome

The verdict was not fixed — it hinged on a holding period and three risk levers, which did **not** matter equally. All three levers grew more powerful the longer the buyer held.

### Holding period — the biggest driver of the advantage
Time amplified everything. The average owner–renter wealth gap rose from **+22 at 5 years to +274 at 20 years**. Ownership's edge came from time in the market, not timing it — the longer the hold, the more decisively buying won.

### Portfolio choice — the dominant risk factor
What the renter invested in was the single most powerful lever in the analysis. Switching the renter from the TSX to the S&P 500 erased most of the ownership advantage, with the swing reaching roughly **811 indexed points at 20-year horizons** (about 40 points at 5 years — a 20-fold increase). It flipped marginal verdicts outright: Toronto's owner win rate fell from a near-certain 90% under the TSX to a 40% coin-flip under the S&P 500, and Montreal's fell from 80% to 20%. The effect was largest for early buyers, who had the most time for the S&P's return edge to compound.

### Renter discipline — meaningful but secondary
A renter who invested only 70% of the cost difference (rather than 100%) handed more advantage to owners. The effect grew with holding period to about **219 indexed points at 20 years** and could tip near-balanced markets — Ottawa swung from a 60% to a 90% owner win rate — but stayed well below the portfolio effect. Vancouver was the least discipline-sensitive market, because strong housing appreciation, not behaviour, drove its result.

### Interest rates — moderate
Applying ±2 percentage-point rate scenarios to the mortgage renewal schedule moved outcomes by up to **~200 indexed points at 20 years**. Pricier markets carried larger mortgages and so were more rate-sensitive — Vancouver showed the greatest sensitivity, Toronto and Montreal the least. Lower rates raised owner win rates in every city and higher rates lowered them; Ottawa swung from 20% (higher rates) to 90% (lower rates), while Vancouver held at 90% throughout — its owner advantage too large for rates to flip.

### Relative magnitude of the three levers
At a 20-year horizon (2005 entry), the three risk factors ranked clearly:

| Risk factor | Comparison | Impact at 20 years (indexed points) |
|---|---|---:|
| Portfolio | S&P 500 vs TSX | ~960 |
| Discipline | 100% vs 70% | ~526 |
| Interest rate | Base +2% vs base | ~336 |

Portfolio choice was roughly double the discipline effect and triple the interest-rate effect. All three grew with the holding period and were roughly comparable only over short horizons.

---

## 6. Patterns by City

- **Vancouver** was the only consistently owner-dominant market — the largest wealth gap (+291) and the least sensitive to behaviour or portfolio choice, because housing appreciation did the heavy lifting.
- **Toronto and Montreal** favoured owning but were highly sensitive to the renter's decisions; a strong renter portfolio could flip them.
- **Calgary, Ottawa, and Edmonton** were near-ties where renting stayed fully competitive. Edmonton was the only market where renting slightly won on average (−10 average gap).

---

## 7. The Verdict

Over 2005–2025, homeownership generally outperformed renting across major Canadian cities under base assumptions — but the advantage was narrow, conditional, and reversible. Owning won in all seven markets on equal capital, yet the national gap was slim (owner 13.5% vs renter 13.2% CAGR), and the outcome depended heavily on city, holding period, and above all on what the renter chose to invest in.

**Why owning won despite weak housing returns.** As a raw asset, Canadian housing was the weakest of the three studied. Ownership still competed because its returns came not from appreciation but from **leverage** (a 20% down payment controlling the full asset), **avoided rent**, and **forced saving**. Housing succeeded as a financial structure, not as a price line.

The rent-versus-buy question has no universal answer. Owning won modestly under typical conditions, but a disciplined renter-investor in a strong portfolio — particularly U.S. or global equities rather than the TSX — could match or beat it. The decision depends less on housing itself than on two behavioural questions: **how long will you stay, and will you actually invest the difference — and in what?**

---

## 8. Caveats and Limitations

- Results reflect the 2005–2025 (and 1990–2025) historical period; past performance does not guarantee future outcomes.
- Conclusions are sensitive to the base assumptions; the sensitivity analysis shows how much each lever moves the result.
- Macro figures (pages 1–4) are real and inflation-adjusted, while simulation figures (pages 5–11) are nominal CAD — the two should not be read on the same scale.
- Housing and equity series use national or benchmark indices that smooth over property-, neighbourhood-, and type-level variation.
- The model assumes consistent renter investing behaviour, which is rarely perfect in practice.
- The ±2 percentage-point mortgage scenarios are hypothetical sensitivity tests, not forecasts.
